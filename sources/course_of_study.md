# A Course of Study

## The Curriculum

A *curriculum* is an engineered structure of information. I understand two types of curriculum: an *imposed curriculum*, which provides resources, a relational framework, expected outputs, grading criteria, etc. in a self-contained package, and a *research curriculum*, which is more of a set of navigational aids, aesthetic criteria, and standards for the production of records, artifacts, and products.

A student is to be forced through an imposed curriculum, fairly rigidly. You can guide yourself through imposed curricula, which is an important skill. The purpose of following an established course of study is to be exposed to structured ways of thinking that have been fruitful in the past. In the context of a community of learners, there are several important outcomes of maintaining imposed curricula: The intersection between the languages of any two individuals is nonempty. Time is not wasted "rediscovering" established ideas. Systems that work well can be maintained efficiently and without reverse engineering or refactoring.

A research curriculum is fundamentally different in that the relevant information is not curated beforehand by an authority. The landscape of information is more "dangerous" (though I hesitate to use such an inflammatory term). Rabbit holes, apathy, delusion, conspiracy, bias, and general disorderliness comprise some of the perils encountered in the depths of research. Each of these is an unrestrained case of a tendency which has merit in moderation.

Assignment 1: Name, and explain the merit of, the restrained cases.

## Degrees of Competence

### Guiding Questions

Can you explain what the problem is?

Can you list tools or techniques that might be useful to solving the problem?

Can you explain how each tool is used?

Can you explain *why* each tool should or should not be used, in context?

Can you demonstrate using each tool to achieve a predefined outcome?

Can you explain the problem as a sequence of subproblems?

Can you explain the problem as a sequence or tree of subproblems, each of which is well-defined? (Can you make a *plan?*)

Can you concentrate on one of those subproblems in a social setting around others who understand the problem?

Can you assign subproblems to competent parties (assess the competence of parties?) intelligently; ensure their communication; set goals, deadlines, incentives, repercussions, and use other social tools to facilitate *coordinated* action? Resolve conflicts, reschedule, redelegate as needed?

### Steps Toward Comprehension

**Can you explain what the problem is?** First, you have to *remember* the problem. Verbatim as it was told to you, if necessary.  To know whether you remember the problem, you must be asked to recall it.

Second, you have to *understand* the problem. What does it mean to understand? The problem is a statement about the configuration of some system; more specifically, about an imperfect alignment between a hypothetical configuration and the observed one. The "system" is not a well-defined, self-contained thing in most cases, but a subset of a complicated network of relations [(note 1)](#1).

To understand something is to be able to answer questions about the content of the problem (by remembering it), as well as questions about the context of the problem, like:

- What would someone who solves problems like this one be called?
- Can you tell a story about a similar problem? (Preferably one with a known solution.)
- Explain the importance of this problem with respect to...
- How will we decide when the problem is to be considered *solved?*

When asked, you should be able to *present,* confidently, clearly, and correctly, a statement of the problem and the context it lives in.
